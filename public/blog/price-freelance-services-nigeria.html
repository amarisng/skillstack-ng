How to Price Your Freelance Services in Nigeria (Without Underselling Yourself)

Most new Nigerian freelancers price by guessing what feels "reasonable," then wonder why they're exhausted and still broke. Pricing isn't a guess — it's a calculation, and most people skip half the inputs.

Why "charge what feels fair" fails

"Fair" is usually anchored to what you'd be comfortable paying yourself, or what a friend charged, neither of which has anything to do with the value you're actually delivering or what it costs you to deliver it. Underpricing doesn't just mean less money — it means taking on more clients than you can serve well, which is the fastest route to burnout and bad reviews.

Start with your real hourly cost, not your ideal one

Work backwards from the income you actually need:

  1. Decide your target monthly income (not revenue — take-home).
  2. Add your business costs: data, software subscriptions, electricity/generator fuel, transport.
  3. Divide by realistic billable hours per month — not 160 (a full-time salary week), because freelancers also spend unpaid time on proposals, admin, and finding clients. 80-100 billable hours/month is more realistic when starting out.
Example: Target take-home ₦300,000/month + ₦40,000 costs = ₦340,000 needed. At 90 billable hours/month, that's roughly ₦3,800/hour minimum — before you've factored in what your specific skill and experience are worth.

This number is your floor, not your rate. It tells you the minimum you can charge and still hit your income target — going below it means working for less than you need, no matter how the client frames the offer.

Then adjust for the market you're selling into

The same skill is worth different amounts to different clients. A logo design might be priced one way for a friend's new business and another way entirely for an established company with a marketing budget. This isn't about charging more just because you can — it's recognising that value is judged by what the client gains, not just your time spent.

  • Local Nigerian small businesses: often price-sensitive, but value speed, reliability, and communication in Pidgin/local context.
  • Nigerian corporates/established brands: larger budgets, but expect more formal process, contracts, and proof of past work.
  • International clients (paid in dollars/pounds): generally the highest ceiling, but expect professionalism matching global freelance platforms and clear async communication.

Package instead of hourly, once you can

Hourly pricing punishes you for getting faster — the better you get at your craft, the less an hourly rate pays for the same result. Project or package pricing (a flat fee for a defined deliverable) rewards skill and speed instead, and is usually easier for clients to say yes to since there's no open-ended bill to worry about.

The real mistake isn't the number — it's not having one

Freelancers who negotiate on the spot, per client, almost always end up lower than freelancers with a rate they calculated once and can quote confidently. Do the math once, write the number down, and treat it as your floor in every conversation from there.
