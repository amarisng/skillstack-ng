Headlines That Earn the Click

Nobody reads a page top to bottom before deciding whether to read it. They scan the headline, decide in about two seconds, and either stay or scroll. The headline isn't decoration — it's the whole pitch.

How readers actually read online

Online reading is scanning, not reading. People skim headlines, subheadings, and the first line of a paragraph, and only slow down to actually read once something has earned their attention. This means a piece of content can be well-written and still fail completely — if the headline doesn't stop the scroll, the writing underneath never gets a chance.

Four headline patterns that work

1. The specific number. "5 mistakes" beats "mistakes to avoid" every time — a number sets a concrete expectation and signals the piece is organized, not rambling.

Weak: "Mistakes Lagos landlords make"
Strong: "5 Mistakes Lagos Landlords Make Before Their First Tenant Moves In"

2. The curiosity gap. Give enough information to create a question, but not enough to answer it. The reader clicks to close the gap.

Weak: "How to save money on data"
Strong: "The Data Plan Trick Most Nigerian Students Don't Know About"

3. The direct benefit. Some audiences don't want curiosity — they want to know exactly what they'll get, especially for practical or financial topics.

Weak: "Improving your business finances"
Strong: "How to Separate Your Business and Personal Money Without Opening a New Account"

4. The bold, specific claim. A clear, almost argumentative statement that makes the reader want to see if you can back it up.

Weak: "Why content marketing matters"
Strong: "Why Your Nigerian Business Doesn't Need a Bigger Ad Budget — It Needs Better Content"

What every strong headline has in common

  • Specificity. A named audience, number, or outcome — not a vague topic.
  • One clear promise. Trying to headline two ideas at once weakens both.
  • Believability. A headline that's too extreme reads as clickbait and gets punished by readers immediately, even if the content is good.

A quick test before you publish

Read your headline on its own, out of context, and ask: would someone who has never heard of this brand still want to know what comes next? If the answer depends on already knowing who you are, the headline is doing the writer's job, not the reader's.
